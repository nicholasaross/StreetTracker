What am I looking at? — what a "journey" is, and how to read these numbers

This site browses everything a single fixed street camera has recorded, organised two ways: a gallery of identified cars and this statistics page for overall traffic and foot traffic.

What is a "journey"?

One journey = one car passing through the camera's view once, in one direction. It is not a trip from A to B, and it is not one car per day. If the same car drives past five times, that's five journeys. If it goes down the street and comes back ten minutes later, that's two journeys (one in each direction). Journeys are counted from the tracker's point of view — a single continuous appearance of a vehicle in frame.

One honest caveat: the tracker occasionally loses a car mid-pass (e.g. it's briefly hidden behind something) and picks it up again as a "new" car, so a single real pass can very occasionally count as two journeys. Counts are close, not exact.

This page vs the gallery

The stats here cast a much wider net than the gallery: they count every car the tracker followed, whether or not a number plate was ever read. That's why these numbers are far larger than the number of gallery cards. The gallery shows only the identifiable minority — cars whose plate was read at least once and matched against the DVSA's public MOT records (that's where make, model, year, colour and fuel type come from). Plates read successfully on very roughly half of passing cars overall: cars travelling one way show the camera their rear plate and read well, cars travelling the other way rarely read at all (camera angle, not plate condition) — so the gallery skews heavily toward one direction of travel. In the gallery, ★ Regular marks a car seen on at least two different calendar dates, and clicking a card opens the car's own page: best photos, DVSA details, a sighting timeline, and an editable notes box ("this is my car", "Nick's car") that persists locally.

Reading the charts

  • Daily journeys by direction — each day's traffic split into left-to-right vs right-to-left. Click a day to see its 15-minute profile.
  • Day-of-week and hour-of-day charts — when the street is busy. All times are the camera's local clock.
  • Speed — estimated from how fast a car crosses the frame{% if stats.speed_unit == 'mph' %}, converted to mph using a single measured road length{% endif %}. Treat it as indicative: it's one global conversion and ignores perspective, so it's good for "faster/slower than typical", not for evidence.
  • Top makes — counts distinct cars, and only plated ones (it needs the DVSA match), so it inherits the gallery's direction skew. Colours and body types count journeys; colour comes from the tracker's own pixels, body type from DVSA where the plate is known and from a shape classifier otherwise.
  • People — the same treatment for pedestrians: one "walk" ≈ one person passing through view once, plus a breakdown into walkers, joggers, cyclists and dog-walkers. (Joggers are split from walkers by speed; cyclist and dog detection were switched on partway through, so older data lumps riders in with joggers and shows zero dog walks.)
  • Out-and-back trips — an estimate: a walk is matched with a later walk in the opposite direction of the same kind (dog walk with dog walk, jog with jog) within a couple of hours, when clothing colours don't disagree. On a busy pavement two different people satisfy that rule constantly, so the headline subtracts a control: the same matching run on time-shuffled data tells us how many pairs pure coincidence produces (roughly three quarters of candidates here), and only the excess is reported as "likely" trips. Matching only ever happens within a single day.
  • Recurring walk patterns — times of day when dog walks, jogs or rides keep happening across many days (e.g. "a dog walk goes left-to-right around 07:50 most mornings"). These are computed purely from timing: nothing links a walk on one day to a walk on another, so a pattern describes the street's rhythm, not a tracked person.

General caveats

  • The camera watches one fixed stretch of one street. Everything here is "as seen from this window", not area-wide traffic data.
  • Coverage isn't continuous — data exists only for the sessions that were recorded and pulled, so quiet periods may mean "not recording" rather than "no traffic". The stats reach further back in time than the photos do, because old images are pruned while the records are kept.
  • No identity tracking of people is done, ever — person analytics are counts, categories and time-of-day patterns. Walks are only ever matched within a single day (to spot out-and-back trips), never across days: a "recurring pattern" is a statistic about the street, not a tracked individual.
